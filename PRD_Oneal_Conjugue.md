# **1\. Product Overview**

**Oneal Conjugue\!** is a French conjugation learning game designed to take a learner from **A1 to C2**, progressively developing the ability to recognise, understand, conjugate, and use French verbs across the full range of relevant **tenses, moods, and conjugation forms**.

The game is not intended to be simply a collection of conjugation quizzes.

Its central goal is:

> **Teach the player how French verbs work, train them to produce the correct forms, and ultimately make them use those forms naturally in context.**

The game combines:

* Structured A1–C2 progression  
* Comprehensive French conjugation coverage  
* Interactive lessons  
* Multiple forms of practice  
* Multiple-choice recognition  
* Active conjugation/production  
* Contextual challenges  
* Mistake tracking and targeted review  
* Mastery progression  
* Stars and rewards  
* Mixed-tense challenges  
* Roleplay simulations  
* Visual and animated scenarios  
* Progressive difficulty  
* Cumulative revision

The ultimate progression is:

> **Learn → Practise → Challenge → Master → Use**

The final stage is particularly important. A player should not only know that *j'ai mangé* is the passé composé of *manger*. They should eventually be able to recognise when passé composé is appropriate and use it naturally in a conversation.

---

# **2\. Product Vision**

Oneal Conjugue\! aims to turn French conjugation from a memorisation-heavy subject into a **progressive game of recognition, recall, decision-making and real-world use**.

The long-term vision is for a learner to be able to enter the game at A1 and progressively develop from:

> **“Which answer is correct?”**

to:

> **“What tense or mood do I need here?”**

and finally:

> **“I can use the appropriate form naturally in this situation.”**

---

# **3\. Problem Statement**

French conjugation can be difficult because learners must simultaneously understand:

* verb endings  
* irregular verb patterns  
* tense formation  
* auxiliary selection  
* agreement  
* spelling changes  
* accents  
* subject/verb relationships  
* tense selection  
* mood selection  
* contextual meaning  
* formal versus informal usage  
* common versus less frequently used forms

Traditional conjugation exercises often focus heavily on memorisation.

A learner may therefore be able to complete:

> Je \_\_\_ (être) → suis

without necessarily being able to decide what form is appropriate in a real sentence.

The game addresses this by progressively moving the player from **recognition to independent production and contextual use**.

# **5\. Core Product Principles**

## **5.1 Recognition is not mastery**

Choosing the correct answer does not automatically mean the player has mastered a conjugation.

The game must progressively require:

1. Recognition  
2. Recall  
3. Conjugation  
4. Contextual understanding  
5. Independent production  
6. Application

---

## **5.2 Mistakes are part of the learning system**

A wrong answer should not simply result in lost points.

The player should understand:

> **Why was my answer wrong?**

and then receive another opportunity to demonstrate the correct understanding.

Repeated mistakes should influence future practice.

---

## **5.3 Previously learned material remains active**

When the player moves to a new tense, old verbs and forms should not disappear.

A verb learned at A1 should continue appearing in later stages.

For example:

> être

may eventually appear across:

* présent  
* passé composé  
* imparfait  
* plus-que-parfait  
* futur simple  
* futur antérieur  
* conditionnel  
* subjonctif  
* and other relevant forms.

This creates cumulative mastery rather than isolated chapter completion.

---

## **5.4 Not every conjugation form has equal practical importance**

The game should recognise the difference between:

* common everyday forms  
* frequently used written forms  
* formal forms  
* advanced forms  
* literary forms  
* forms primarily useful for recognition rather than everyday production

Common forms should receive extensive production and contextual practice.

Less frequently used forms can place greater emphasis on recognition, comprehension and appropriate selective production.

# **6\. Learning Progression**

The core progression is:

> **Learn → Practise → Challenge → Master → Simulation**

Each major tense, mood, or conjugation area follows this progression.

---

# **7\. Stage 1 — Learn**

The player receives a concise explanation of the new concept.

A lesson should explain:

* What the tense/mood/form is  
* When it is used  
* How it is formed  
* Important rules  
* Common patterns  
* Important irregularities  
* Example sentences  
* Relevant conjugation tables

The lesson should not become a long grammar textbook.

The objective is to give the player enough understanding to begin practising.

---

# **8\. Stage 2 — Practise**

The player begins guided exercises.

Practice can include:

### **Recognition**

> Je \_\_\_ au travail tous les jours.  
> suis / es / est / sont

### **Conjugation construction**

The player builds the correct form from its components.

### **Table completion**

> Je suis  
> Tu \_\_\_  
> Il est  
> Nous sommes

### **Guided sentence completion**

The player completes sentences using the target tense.

### **Controlled production**

The player produces the correct conjugation without being given all possible answers.

The game gradually reduces assistance as the player improves.

---

# **9\. Stage 3 — Challenge**

The player is expected to demonstrate the skill with less assistance.

Challenges can involve:

* unfamiliar verbs  
* irregular verbs  
* different subjects  
* affirmative sentences  
* negative sentences  
* questions  
* different sentence structures  
* different contexts  
* distractor answers  
* spelling traps  
* accent traps  
* tense traps  
* confusing verb forms

At this stage, the player should increasingly have to **think rather than recognise**.

---

# **10\. Stage 4 — Master**

A tense or mood is considered mastered only after the player demonstrates competence across multiple dimensions.

Mastery should consider:

* identifying the form  
* understanding its usage  
* conjugating correctly  
* handling different verbs  
* handling different subjects  
* handling regular verbs  
* handling irregular verbs  
* using the form in context  
* producing the form independently  
* avoiding recurring mistakes

A player should therefore not be able to achieve mastery simply by repeatedly memorising one set of questions.

---

# **11\. Stage 5 — Simulation**

Once the required mastery milestone has been reached, the player can unlock a contextual roleplay simulation.

The simulation is the bridge between:

> **“I know this conjugation.”**

and

> **“I can actually use this French.”**

The simulation should place the player inside a realistic situation supported by appropriate **graphics, characters, animations and visual context**.

Examples include:

* Restaurant  
* Airport  
* Hotel  
* Shopping  
* Workplace  
* Job interview  
* University  
* Doctor's appointment  
* Travel  
* Meeting someone  
* Making arrangements  
* Solving a problem  
* Formal discussion  
* Debate  
* Social conversation

The player interacts with the scenario and must produce appropriate French.

The game should not always explicitly tell the player which tense to use.

Instead, the situation should provide enough context for the player to determine what form is appropriate.

---

# **12\. Simulation Progression**

Simulations should become progressively more complex.

### **Early levels**

The required conjugation may be obvious.

### **Intermediate levels**

Several previously learned tenses may be relevant.

### **Advanced levels**

The player must determine the appropriate tense or mood from context.

### **C1–C2**

The simulation can involve:

* formal communication  
* hypothetical situations  
* expressing doubt  
* expressing necessity  
* expressing emotion  
* argumentation  
* reported situations  
* complex temporal relationships  
* formal and literary language  
* nuanced distinctions between moods and tenses

The simulation should therefore test **application**, not merely conjugation recall.

---

# **13\. CEFR Progression**

The game will follow:

> **A1 → A2 → B1 → B2 → C1 → C2**

Progression through the CEFR levels is structured, but individual tense/mood progression within a level is **mastery-based**.

A player does not simply complete a chapter because they answered enough questions once.

Instead:

> **CEFR progression \+ mastery progression**

work together.

---

# **14\. Conjugation Curriculum**

The game should cover the major French conjugation system rather than limiting itself to a small fixed list of tenses.

The curriculum should include relevant areas such as:

## **Indicatif**

Including forms such as:

* Présent  
* Passé composé  
* Imparfait  
* Plus-que-parfait  
* Futur simple  
* Futur antérieur  
* Passé simple  
* Other appropriate indicative forms

## **Futur constructions**

Including:

* Futur proche  
* Futur simple  
* Futur antérieur

## **Conditionnel**

Including:

* Conditionnel présent  
* Conditionnel passé

## **Subjonctif**

Including relevant forms such as:

* Subjonctif présent  
* Subjonctif passé  
* Advanced/literary subjunctive forms where appropriate

## **Impératif**

Including relevant imperative forms.

## **Other Conjugation Forms and Constructions**

Where appropriate to the learner's level, the curriculum should include:

* Infinitif présent  
* Infinitif passé  
* Participe présent  
* Participe passé  
* Gérondif  
* Passive constructions  
* Relevant compound constructions  
* Other important French conjugation patterns

The exact placement of each form across A1–C2 should be determined by its **language-learning relevance, frequency, difficulty and expected use at that level**.

# **15\. Verb Progression**

Verb selection should combine three principles:

### **15.1 Frequency**

Common French verbs should appear frequently.

Examples include:

* être  
* avoir  
* aller  
* faire  
* pouvoir  
* vouloir  
* devoir  
* savoir  
* venir  
* prendre

### **15.2 Verb Families**

The game should expose patterns shared by related verbs.

Examples:

> prendre → apprendre → comprendre

> venir → revenir → devenir

> mettre → permettre → promettre

This helps players understand that conjugation can involve reusable patterns.

### **15.3 Regular and Irregular Progression**

The player should encounter regular verbs before progressively facing more difficult irregular patterns.

However, irregular verbs should also appear early where they are essential to basic French.

Previously learned verbs should continue returning at higher levels.

---

# **16\. Practice Modes**

The game should use a mixture of exercise types rather than relying on one question format.

Possible exercise types include:

### **Multiple Choice**

The player selects the correct form.

### **Fill in the Blank**

The player produces the missing conjugation.

### **Conjugation Table**

The player completes missing forms.

### **Sentence Transformation**

The player changes a sentence into a requested tense or structure.

### **Context Selection**

The player determines which tense or mood is appropriate.

### **Error Detection**

The player identifies the incorrect conjugation.

### **Correction**

The player fixes an incorrect sentence.

### **Mixed Tense Challenge**

The player receives sentences containing previously learned forms without being told which tense or mood to use.

### **Production Challenge**

The player produces the required conjugation independently.

The game should introduce these modes progressively rather than overwhelming beginners.

---

# **17\. Mixed Tense Challenges**

Mixed challenges are an essential part of the game.

Once several forms have been mastered, the player should encounter questions where the target tense or mood is **not announced**.

For example, after learning:

* Présent  
* Passé composé  
* Imparfait  
* Futur proche  
* Futur simple

the player may encounter:

> Quand j'étais petit, je \_\_\_ souvent au parc.

The player must determine that **imparfait** is required.

Later:

> Hier, nous \_\_\_ au cinéma.

The player must determine that **passé composé** is appropriate.

Eventually, mixed challenges can contain many previously learned forms.

---

# **18\. Mistake System**

Every meaningful mistake should become a learning opportunity.

When the player answers incorrectly:

### **Step 1 — Explain**

The game clearly explains:

> Why the selected answer is wrong.

The explanation should be specific rather than simply saying:

> “Incorrect.”

### **Step 2 — Corrective Practice**

The player receives another related question or opportunity to demonstrate the correct concept.

### **Step 3 — Mistake Tracking**

The mistake is remembered as an area requiring additional practice.

### **Step 4 — Future Review**

The weak area should return naturally in later practice.

The goal is to prevent the player from repeatedly making the same error without intervention.

---

# **19\. Mistake Categories**

Mistakes can be associated with areas such as:

* Wrong tense  
* Wrong mood  
* Wrong subject conjugation  
* Wrong verb ending  
* Wrong auxiliary  
* Wrong agreement  
* Spelling  
* Accent  
* Irregular conjugation  
* Confusion between similar forms  
* Incorrect contextual usage

This allows the game to provide meaningful feedback about **what the player actually struggles with**.

---

# **20\. Stars**

Stars are the game's primary visible reward system.

A chapter or challenge can award:

> ⭐  
> ⭐⭐  
> ⭐⭐⭐

Stars represent **performance and achievement**, rather than being the same thing as mastery.

A player can therefore have:

> **Passé composé**  
> ⭐⭐⭐ Stars  
> **Mastery: 78%**

This distinction prevents a high score on one activity from being treated as complete linguistic mastery.

---

# **21\. Mastery System**

Mastery is separate from stars.

A player's mastery can reflect performance across:

* Recognition  
* Conjugation  
* Context  
* Production  
* Regular verbs  
* Irregular verbs  
* Different subjects  
* Different sentence structures  
* Mistake frequency  
* Retention over time

The player should be able to see areas of strength and weakness.

Example:

> **Passé composé**

> Recognition: Strong  
> Conjugation: Strong  
> Context: Developing  
> Production: Developing  
> Irregular verbs: Strong

The exact presentation can evolve with the game.

---

# **22\. Unlocking System**

The game uses **CEFR \+ mastery-based progression**.

The player progresses through:

> A1 → A2 → B1 → B2 → C1 → C2

Within each CEFR level, individual topics become available progressively.

A simplified progression could be:

> Learn  
> ↓  
> Practise  
> ↓  
> Challenge  
> ↓  
> Mastery milestone  
> ↓  
> ⭐ Achievement  
> ↓  
> New content / challenge  
> ↓  
> Simulation unlock

Previously learned material remains available and continues to appear in revision.

---

# **23\. Simulation Unlocks**

Roleplay simulations should feel like a meaningful reward rather than simply another exercise.

A simulation may require:

* mastery of one tense/mood  
* mastery of several related forms  
* a certain number of stars  
* successful completion of a challenge  
* or a combination of these

For example:

> **Master Passé composé**  
> ⭐⭐⭐  
> 🔓 **Simulation: Une journée à Paris**

Later:

> **Master Passé composé \+ Imparfait \+ Futur**  
> ⭐⭐⭐ in each  
> 🔓 **Simulation: Raconter sa vie**

Advanced simulations may require mastery of an entire cluster of grammatical forms.

# **24\. Roleplay Simulation System**

The roleplay system should eventually become one of the game's major features.

Each simulation should have:

### **A Situation**

A realistic scenario.

### **Characters**

Characters with personalities and different conversational roles.

### **Visual Environment**

Appropriate locations and visual context.

### **Dialogue**

Natural French interactions.

### **Player Responses**

The player must respond appropriately.

### **Conjugation Requirements**

The scenario naturally creates opportunities to use target tenses and moods.

### **Consequences**

The conversation can respond differently depending on the player's answer.

The objective is to make the player feel that they are **using French**, rather than completing another worksheet.

# **25\. Example Simulation**

### **Scenario: Job Interview**

The player has mastered:

* Présent  
* Passé composé  
* Imparfait  
* Futur  
* Conditionnel

The interviewer asks:

> **Pouvez-vous me parler de votre expérience professionnelle ?**

The player needs to describe previous experience.

Later:

> **Qu'est-ce que vous faisiez dans votre ancien poste ?**

The player must naturally use an appropriate past form.

Then:

> **Que feriez-vous si vous rencontriez un problème avec un client ?**

The player must handle a conditional situation.

The game is therefore testing:

> **Can the player choose and use the appropriate conjugation in context?**

rather than:

> **Can the player memorise the conditional ending?**

# **27\. Deliberate Traps**

The existing game concept of deliberate traps should remain a central feature.

Wrong answers should reflect realistic learner mistakes, such as:

* Wrong accent  
* Wrong spelling  
* Wrong tense  
* Wrong subject  
* Wrong ending  
* Wrong auxiliary  
* Similar-looking conjugation  
* Correct conjugation used in the wrong context  
* Confusion between indicative and subjunctive  
* Confusion between conditional and future

The purpose of a trap is not to trick the player unfairly.

It should represent a mistake that a learner could realistically make.

---

# **28\. Explanations**

Explanations should answer:

> **Why is this answer correct?**

and, when the player chooses incorrectly:

> **Why is my answer wrong?**

Where useful, explanations can include:

* Conjugation table  
* Relevant rule  
* Comparison with the incorrect form  
* Example sentence  
* Usage explanation  
* Reminder of a previous mistake

The existing principle that explanations should remain available until the player acknowledges them should be retained.

---

# **29\. Contextual Learning**

Sentences should increasingly represent meaningful situations rather than isolated conjugation drills.

Examples:

### **A1**

> Je \_\_\_ à Lagos.

### **A2**

> Hier, j'\_\_\_ travaillé toute la journée.

### **B1**

> Si j'avais plus de temps, je \_\_\_ davantage.

### **B2**

> Il fallait que nous \_\_\_ avant midi.

### **C1**

> Bien qu'il \_\_\_ déjà terminé son travail...

### **C2**

More complex formal, literary or nuanced contexts.

The objective is to connect **form, meaning and situation**.

---

# **30\. Audio and Pronunciation**

French pronunciation should eventually be integrated into the learning experience.

Where appropriate, the player should be able to hear:

* The correct conjugated sentence  
* Important pronunciation contrasts  
* The sentence used in context

Audio should complement conjugation rather than become a separate pronunciation course.

---

# **31\. Translation Support**

English translations can support comprehension, particularly at lower levels.

However, translations should gradually become less central as the player's French level increases.

The intended progression is:

> **A1:** French \+ strong English support  
> **A2:** French \+ moderate English support  
> **B1:** French-first  
> **B2:** Mostly French  
> **C1–C2:** Primarily French

The objective is to prevent the player from becoming dependent on English.

# **40\. Product Differentiator**

The central differentiator of Oneal Conjugue\! is:

> **It does not stop at asking whether the player knows a conjugation. It progressively asks whether the player can recognise it, produce it, choose it, and use it.**

> **Recognise → Understand → Conjugate → Recall → Choose → Produce → Use**

The roleplay simulation is the final expression of that progression.

# **44\. Product North Star**

The ultimate test of Oneal Conjugue\! should be:

> **If a player has mastered a tense in the game, can they recognise it, conjugate it correctly, understand why it is used, and use it appropriately when placed in a realistic French situation?**

# **45\. Final Product Structure**

The complete product can be understood as five connected layers:

## **1\. 📚 Learn**

Understand the tense, mood or conjugation form.

## **2\. 🎮 Practise**

Build recognition and recall.

## **3\. 🧠 Master**

Demonstrate independent knowledge and correct recurring mistakes.

## **4\. 🏆 Progress**

Earn stars, unlock content and advance through A1–C2.

## **5\. 🎭 Use**

Apply the language in realistic animated roleplay simulations.

**Oneal Conjugue\! is therefore not simply a French conjugation quiz.**

It is a **progressive French conjugation mastery game that takes the player from learning individual verb forms to using them appropriately in context.**
